Job Market Paper

Probabilistic Narrative Identification: Evidence from Tax Policy

Winner of the International Association for Applied Econometrics (IAAE) 2026 Best Graduate Paper Award
job market paper online appendix

This paper develops a probabilistic approach to narrative identification. Traditional narrative studies retain policy actions classified as exogenous and exclude the remainder from identification. I instead assign each action a probability of exogeneity, allowing the broader policy record to remain informative. Variation in this probability allows policy actions to be reweighted so that the endogenous component cancels, recovering the component associated with exogenous policy. I construct these probabilities through a replicable mapping from policy documents using large language models. Applied to the canonical tax-policy sample of Romer and Romer (2010), the framework yields an estimated medium-run GDP contraction about two-thirds smaller than under binary selection. I then extend the analysis to indirect taxation by constructing two new datasets of U.S. budget and French VAT proposals. VAT illustrates how probabilistic identification makes narrative analysis feasible when binary selection leaves too little credible identifying variation. Together, the applications reveal contrasting transmission patterns: U.S. tax increases lower output and inflation, with similar consumption and investment contributions to the GDP decline, whereas VAT increases initially raise prices and contract output, with consumption contributing more than investment.

Local Linear Local Projections: Estimating Shock-Size Heterogeneity

How do the effects of economic shocks vary with their size and sign? This paper develops a flexible method to study nonlinear shock responses and clarifies when the estimates have a causal interpretation.

paper

Do large shocks propagate differently from small ones, and do positive and negative shocks have the same effects? A nonlinear response to the initial shock can reflect either a direct nonlinear effect of the shock or propagation through economic conditions changed by the shock. I show that these mechanisms are generally observationally equivalent from the response curve alone: distinguishing them requires additional information on the propagation mechanism. Rather than seeking to identify which mechanism generates the nonlinearity, I take a local perspective, asking how the response to a marginal change in the shock varies across shock values and when this response has a causal interpretation. I propose Local Linear Local Projections (LL-LP) to trace this variation without imposing bins, thresholds, or a global nonlinear functional form. I show how conventional linear projections and specifications that split shocks by sign or size provide different summaries of the underlying slope curve. The framework also provides a basis for testing sign and size nonlinearities separately while allowing the shock distribution to be asymmetric.

Earlier versions were presented or circulated as: Fiscal Multiplier: the Size of the Shock Matters.

Fiscal Policy and Inflation: Non-Linearities in Government Debt (2024)

with C. Checherita-Westphal | ECB Working Paper
paper

This paper investigates the interplay between discretionary fiscal policy and inflation in the euro area, emphasizing the role of public debt levels in modulating this relationship. It explores how fiscal expansions or contractions influence inflationary pressures, particularly under varying debt conditions. The analysis reveals that fiscal policy’s effect on inflation is non-linear, with debt levels significantly affecting the inflationary outcome of fiscal measures. High debt levels tend to amplify the inflation response to fiscal expansions, a finding that holds under multiple analytical frameworks and robustness checks. This paper contributes to the empirical literature by highlighting the critical role of fiscal policy, especially in high-debt environments, and its implications for inflation dynamics in the euro area.
